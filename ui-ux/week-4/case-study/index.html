Hisaab: settling hostel and trip expenses without the awkwardness

Role: solo UI/UX designer (research, IA, interaction and visual design, prototyping, handoff) · Timeline: 1 week · Platform: Android and iOS (mobile-first) · Tools: HTML/CSS design-in-code, Playwright for exports, axe-core for accessibility

1. The problem

In a hostel room or on a group trip, money moves in small, frequent amounts: a ₹360 Maggi run, a ₹480 cab, the month's Wi-Fi recharge. Paying is effortless now, because almost every student has UPI (India processed about 21,860 crore UPI transactions in FY26 [1]). Keeping track of who owes whom is not. It lives in screenshots, notes apps and memory, and asking a friend for ₹120 feels petty enough that people stop asking.

How might we let a group of friends record shared expenses in seconds and settle them over UPI, without anyone having to play accountant or debt collector?

2. Research

Constraint: one week and no budget for interviews, so this was secondary research plus proto-personas, labelled as assumptions to validate (see section 9).

  • Market: UPI is the default payment rail for students, and peer-to-peer transfers are instant and free. The gap is bookkeeping, not payment.
  • Review mining: the main Splitwise complaints in app-store review analyses are recent restrictions on the free plan and pushes towards Pro [2]. Competitors report a daily cap on new expenses for free users. That claim comes from interested parties and couldn't be verified independently [3].
  • Payment apps: Google Pay's Split expense splits one bill you paid. Only the creator receives money, and there is no running balance across many expenses [4]. That works for one dinner, not a semester in Room 214.

3. Design principles

  1. Ten seconds or it won't happen: adding an expense starts with the amount keypad, and smart defaults do the rest (paid by me, split equally, everyone in the group).
  2. One number per relationship: people care about "what do I owe", not the ledger. The ledger is one tap away.
  3. The app asks for money, not you: neutral wording, user-controlled reminders, never automatic nagging.
  4. Never touch the money: UPI intents only. This is said plainly on screen, because trust is the product.

4. Information architecture and key flows

Tabs: Home (overall balance + groups) · Groups · Add · Activity · You. The three critical flows were wireframed first, in greyscale, to settle structure before visuals:

  • A. Onboard: value → split → settle promise → phone sign-in
  • B. Add an expense: FAB → amount → split → save → confirmation
  • C. Settle up: balance → simplified payments → UPI → success with the updated balance

5. Design system

  • Colour: a calm teal (trust, not "bank blue"), plus semantic owe (red) / owed (green) with tints, amber for nudges, and a neutral ink ramp.
  • Type: Inter Display for numbers and titles (amounts are the hero, up to 52 px / 800), Inter for text, on a 1.25 scale.
  • Spacing and radius: a 4/8 grid, with 16 px gutters and 12–20 px radii.
  • Accessibility: all 15 colour pairs pass WCAG AA (contrast.md), and amounts are never colour-only: every red or green number carries "you owe" or "owes you".
  • Components: balance hero, group row, expense row, amount pill, segmented split control, keypad, FAB, payment option, success state.

6. High-fidelity screens (18)

Onboarding ×3, sign-in, home, group, add expense, split options, saved, expense detail, balances & simplify, settle up, payment success, create group & invite, activity, monthly insights, profile, friendly reminder. See the board at the top and exports/screens/.

Some decisions worth calling out: * Home leads with one signed number (+ ₹854) and two actions, Settle up and Remind. Diya's and Kabir's main jobs are one tap away. * Split options default to "Equally" with checkboxes, because the most common exception is "someone wasn't there", not percentages. The running total ("₹120 each · 3 people · Totals ₹360 ✓") prevents maths doubts. * Simplify debts shows the saving ("5 payments → 3"), so people understand why the suggested payments differ from the raw ledger. * Settle up names the payee's UPI ID, what the payment is for, and the reassurance "Paid directly bank to bank". * The success screen shows the new balance ("You owe ₹120, was ₹386") and offers the next payment. One session can clear everything. * Reminders come in three tones and are sent from the user's own WhatsApp: the awkward part becomes a choice, not a chore.

7. Prototype

prototype.html links all 18 screens. The three key flows are fully clickable, with push and back transitions and keyboard support (walkthrough video).

8. Evaluation so far

  • Accessibility audit (axe-core 4.10, WCAG 2.2 AA): the first run found 12 tappable icons that were plain divs with labels. They became real focusable buttons with names, and the screens and prototype now report 0 violations. The same audit, run on my week-3 dashboard, caught contrast failures that my own token table had missed. Lesson: compute and audit.
  • Cognitive walkthrough of the three key flows against the personas' goals:
  • add expense: 4 taps with defaults;
  • settle a debt from Home: 3 taps plus the UPI app;
  • one issue found and fixed in the prototype: after saving, Back returned to the filled-in form, which invites duplicate expenses. Back now goes Home once a flow has finished.

9. What I'd validate next

  • 5 moderated usability tests with hostel students, giving each the three flows as tasks. Success metrics: add an expense in under 15 s; correctly identify "what do I owe Diya?"; no one doubts that an equal split is fair.
  • Reminder tone A/B test: does "Friendly" get paid faster than "Direct"?
  • Risky assumption: that users are comfortable with Hisaab knowing their UPI ID. Test the copy and a "record cash payment" fallback.

10. Outcome

A complete, developer-ready design: * 18 screens and a clickable prototype; * a token-based design system; * a handoff package with specs, assets, behaviour rules and a screen inventory (handoff.md); * onboarding UX copy with its rationale (ux-copy.md).

Biggest learning: the hard part of splitting expenses is social, not mathematical. The best design decisions here (one number, user-sent reminders, "never holds money") came from naming the awkwardness, not the arithmetic.
